A short, printable guide

The four assistants & what they're for.

There are a lot of AI tools now, and most of the noise about them is wrong in opposite directions. This guide names the four assistants you'll actually meet, the harnesses that bolt them to real work, and the words you need to follow a conversation. Read it once. Keep it on the desk. Bring it back when the next thing launches — and it will.

§ 01 The four assistants

Same idea. Different houses.

Each of these is a chat interface that sits on top of a large language model. They will all answer most questions. They differ in tone, in what they will not say, and in what they are quietly best at. Pick by job, not by loyalty.

Anthropic
Claude
claude.ai

Best for Long-form thinking, nuanced reasoning, writing that needs care, theology that needs patience.

Strength Will tell you when it doesn't know. Most willing to sit with a hard question instead of resolving it.

Watch for Defaults can lean cautious and progressive — system instructions move the needle if you set them.

OpenAI
ChatGPT
chatgpt.com

Best for A general everyday assistant. Voice mode is the best of the four. Built-in image generation works without ceremony.

Strength The most polished consumer experience. The widest ecosystem (Custom GPTs, plugins, mobile app).

Watch for A recognizable "house voice." Same political default tilt as Claude unless you steer it.

xAI
Grok — the pick for one-offs
grok.com

Best for One-off prompts. Current events. Anything that benefits from live search or reading what's happening on X.

Strength The most search-native of the four. Tends most neutral politically and spiritually at default settings. Supports skills.

Watch for Smaller ecosystem. Fewer mature integrations than the older players.

Google
Gemini
gemini.google.com

Best for Creative quick-starts. First drafts of images. Sitting inside Gmail, Docs, and the rest of the Google stack.

Strength Fast, strong on images and video, native to where your calendar and inbox already live.

Watch for Less consistent for deep reasoning. Can be quick to hedge.

For most of what we do — searching, checking, asking a single good question — Grok is the workhorse. It's the most neutral at defaults, the most search-biased, and the fastest path from question to a useful answer. The short version
§ 02 Harnesses — when chat isn't enough

For the work, not the question.

A harness is the scaffolding around a model. It gives the model tools — your files, your calendar, the web, a terminal — and a place to do real work without you copy-pasting every step. If you're doing sermon prep, planning a series, running a weekly check-in, or wiring up a verse-of-the-day flow, you want a harness, not a chat tab.

For everyday work

Cowork — most accessible

Bundles AI with the apps you already use — calendar, email, notes, browser. Plugins for specific jobs. Closest thing to "AI in your real life" without a learning curve.

For text-shaped projects

Claude Code

A terminal harness from Anthropic. Originally for coding; equally good at sermon outlines, study notes, multi-file projects. Reads and writes files on your machine. Skills, hooks, and slash commands.

For coding tasks

Codex

OpenAI's coding agent. Sits in your editor or runs in the cloud against a repo. If your "work" is software, this is the one. Otherwise pass.

The rule of thumb: if you'd do it more than once, give it a harness. A verse-of-the-day, a Sunday-morning check-in, a monthly review of the sermon series — those want a harness with a scheduled task and a skill. A single question on a Tuesday wants a chat tab.

You are my sermon-prep second mind. Each week I'll paste the passage. Give me: three questions the text resists, one image I haven't seen before, and one place I'm tempted to soften the edge. Don't give applications until I ask.
§ 03 Image models — a footnote

Useful. Rarely necessary.

Most pastoral work doesn't need a picture. But when it does — a slide, a social post, a study-guide cover — these are the tools. Treat them as the spice rack, not the meal.

Inside ChatGPT
DALL·E / GPT Image

Lowest friction. Just ask. Good enough for slides and social.

Inside Gemini
Imagen / Nano Banana

Strong on photoreal and on iterative edits ("now make it dusk").

Standalone
Midjourney

The artist's pick. Steeper learning curve. The best-looking output.

Standalone
Ideogram

The one that gets typography right. Use it when there are words in the image.

§ 04 Glossary

The vocabulary, plainly.

If a word in the conversation made you nod and quietly resolve to look it up later — start here. Beginner terms first, intermediate after.

PromptBeginner

What you type into the model. The art is asking a question you hadn't thought to ask — not a vague request.

TokenBeginner

The unit a model thinks in — a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation mark. Roughly four characters of English. Models charge per token and remember in tokens.

Context windowBeginner

How much the model can hold in mind at once. Bigger windows mean it can read a whole book; smaller windows mean it forgets the top of the conversation by the bottom.

System promptBeginner

The instructions the model gets before you say anything — its rules of engagement. Most products have one baked in. When you set your own, you change its personality.

HallucinationBeginner

When the model says something confidently false. It isn't lying; it's pattern-matching past the edge of what it knows. Verify anything that matters.

MultimodalBeginner

A model that handles more than text — images, audio, video. All four assistants are multimodal now to varying degrees.

HarnessIntermediate

The scaffolding around a model that gives it tools and a place to work — Cowork, Claude Code, Codex. The model is the engine; the harness is the car.

TransformerIntermediate

The architecture under every modern AI assistant. Introduced in 2017. Its trick is paying attention to every word in the input at once instead of reading left-to-right.

Pre-trainingIntermediate

The long, expensive phase where a fresh model reads a sizable fraction of the internet and learns the shape of language. Months of compute. Done once per model.

Post-trainingIntermediate

Everything done after pre-training to make a raw model into a useful assistant — instruction tuning, safety work, RLHF. Where most of the "personality" gets installed.

Fine-tuningIntermediate

Taking a finished model and training it further on a narrower set of examples — your sermons, your style, your knowledge base — to specialize it.

RLHFIntermediate

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. The most common post-training technique. Humans rate two answers; the model learns to prefer the higher-rated one. How the assistants get their manners.

InferenceIntermediate

The act of running the model after it's trained — generating an answer. What you pay for when you use an API.

Coding agentIntermediate

A harness that lets the model actually do things — write files, run commands, hit the web — instead of just suggesting them. Claude Code and Codex are coding agents; Cowork bundles them for non-coders.

SkillIntermediate

A packaged set of instructions (and sometimes code) a harness can load on demand. A reusable recipe — "draft a sermon outline," "summarize this meeting" — that the model picks up when relevant.

MCPIntermediate

Model Context Protocol. The open standard for plugging tools into harnesses — your calendar, your notes app, a database. If a tool "supports MCP," any modern harness can use it.
